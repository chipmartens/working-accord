The ten rules

The Working Accord is ten rules for bringing AI into a workplace fairly. A person signs off on anything that matters. Nobody is secretly scored by software. Time the software saves is shared in writing. Anyone whose job will change or go hears first, with notice, retraining and severance. Employers sign all ten or none.

Version 1.0, October 2026. Free to use, copy and adapt.

  1. 01

    Machines take the repetition. People keep the judgment.

    Software takes on repetitive tasks; decisions that need judgment stay with people.

    What it asks
    List which tasks the software will do and which decisions stay with people, before it goes in.
    Why
    Repetitive work is real work, and for some people it's the whole job. Naming the tasks up front is how you find out whose job changes, which is what rules 8 and 9 are for.
  2. 02

    A person signs off on anything that matters.

    Decisions about safety, money, health or people's jobs need a named person's approval, not just a model's output.

    What it asks
    The person signing can overrule the software, gets enough time to actually check its work, and is never penalized for saying no.
    Why
    A sign-off without time or authority is a rubber stamp. In one study, consultants who leaned on AI for a task it was weak at got the right answer 19 points less often than those who didn't.4
  3. 03

    Questioning the tool is part of the job.

    Raising a concern about an AI tool, with reasons, counts as doing the job well.

    What it asks
    A clear way to flag that a tool is wrong, unsafe, a privacy risk or a bad fit, and a response within a set time.
    Why
    The people closest to the work see the failures first. If questioning the tool looks like resisting change, they stop saying anything.
  4. 04

    Show the work, the machine's and yours.

    Say what the software produced and what you added, at every level of the organization.

    What it asks
    Nobody passes off a machine's draft as their own thinking, starting with the people in charge.
    Why
    Credit and blame should land on the right place. It also tells the next person how much to trust the result.
  5. 05

    Keep the skill to check it.

    People must stay able to do and judge the work themselves, so someone can tell when the software is wrong.

    What it asks
    New hires learn the job itself, not just how to prompt for it. Teams practise the task without the tool often enough to keep the skill.
    Why
    Feeling faster isn't being faster. In a 2025 trial, experienced programmers took 19% longer with AI tools while believing they'd been about 20% faster.3
  6. 06

    No secret scorekeeping.

    No one is disciplined or laid off because software ranked them, and nobody is watched or ranked by software without being told.

    What it asks
    A software ranking is never the reason for discipline or a layoff; the person deciding must be able to explain the decision without it. Where software watches or ranks people at all, they're told first and shown what it says about them, with a way to correct it.
    Why
    People can't fix a record they can't see. Canada's federal Privacy Commissioner says workplace monitoring should be "specific, targeted and appropriate in the circumstances." This rule adds that people are told.20
  7. 07

    Your know-how is not free training data.

    When an experienced worker's knowledge is used to train a system, they're told, credited and paid for it.

    What it asks
    Tell people before their work is used to train a system, name them where it's used, and pay for it as the valuable work it is.
    Why
    In a field study of 5,172 support agents, an assistant learned from the best support agents' conversations and lifted everyone else. Nothing in the study says the best agents were paid more for it.1
  8. 08

    Time saved gets shared, in writing.

    Before software goes in, the team and management agree in writing how freed-up hours will be used, and at least half go back to the people doing the work.

    What it asks
    At least half of the saved time goes to higher pay, paid training or better work. Fewer overtime hours only count if take-home pay holds. The split is reported to the team every quarter.
    How to count
    Before the software goes in, record how long the affected tasks take per unit of work (per order, case, report or part), from three months of records, or a two-week time log if there are none. Each month after, measure the same tasks the same way. Hours saved = the time per unit you no longer spend, times this month's volume. Turn hours into dollars at the team's average hourly rate, so pay, training and new work can be added up together. At least half of that amount goes to the team. A person the team chooses checks the count and decides what counts as "better work". Report it on one page every quarter, for at least 12 months. Disagreements go to the accord's breach process.
    Why
    Gains don't reach paycheques on their own. A large Danish study found AI chatbots had no measurable effect yet on average earnings or hours.10
  9. 09

    If a job is going to change or go, the person hears first.

    Anyone whose role will change or end because of software gets notice, retraining and severance before it happens.

    What it asks
    At least 90 days' notice, or the legal minimum if that is longer. First claim on any new role the change creates. Paid retraining. Severance above the legal minimum, on top of retraining, not instead of it.
    Why
    When telephone exchanges switched to dial, operators over 25 were about 7 points less likely to be working ten years later.7 The cost of a transition lands hardest on the people already in the job.
  10. 10

    A headcount number is not a plan.

    Decide what the work should look like before deciding who does it.

    What it asks
    No AI project is approved on a headcount target alone. The plan names the work, the people affected, and how rules 8 and 9 will be met.
    Why
    A layoff lands on people and families, not on a spreadsheet. And a plan built only on a smaller payroll usually skips the work of finding out what the software is actually good at.

Signing the accord. Workers sign to say these rules are the floor. Employers sign all ten as a commitment and are listed with the date. Sign the accord or read the employer guide.
