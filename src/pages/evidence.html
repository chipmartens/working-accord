The work comes back. Not always for the same people.

History shows new technology creates more work than it destroys, but not for the people who lose the old work, and not quickly. Handloom weavers' pay fell to about a quarter of its peak. Older telephone operators were less likely to be working a decade after dial phones. Today, young workers in AI-exposed jobs are being hired less.

How many Canadian jobs are exposed to AI?

Among Canadian employees aged 18 to 64, about 31% work in jobs highly exposed to AI with low "complementarity", meaning some of their tasks could be replaced by AI in future. Another 29% are in highly exposed jobs where AI is more likely to support the work, and about 40% are in jobs that aren't highly exposed. Statistics Canada estimated this from 2021 census data and published it in 2024. "Highly exposed" means above the median for all occupations.

Exposure is not the same as job loss. It measures how much of a job's tasks AI could do, not what employers will choose to do with it.19

Is AI causing job losses in Canada?

There is no official Canadian count of jobs lost to AI yet. The clearest early signal comes from US payroll data: employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed jobs fell 13% relative to less-exposed jobs in the first release, widening to 19% in the 2026 update, mostly through less hiring. Experienced workers showed no gap.9

The authors call this a warning sign, not proof that AI caused it. Some jobs will be lost to AI. The open question is who carries the cost.

Does new technology create more jobs than it destroys?

Over long periods, yes. About 60% of US jobs in 2018 had titles that didn't exist in 1940.18 In 1966 a US presidential commission concluded that "technology eliminates jobs, not work."15

But the same research shows where the new work went. Since 1980 it has been mostly well-paid professional jobs and low-paid service jobs, while middle-paying jobs thinned. The 1966 commission itself recommended public service jobs, a guaranteed minimum income and education for people displaced in the meantime.

What happened to weavers during the Industrial Revolution?

Handloom weavers' weekly pay fell from about 20 shillings in 1806 to about 6 by 1830, and their real wages fell to roughly a quarter of the peak.5 Across Britain, output per worker rose 46% between 1780 and 1840 while real wages rose 12%. Wages took until the second half of the century to catch up, about two generations.6

What happened to telephone operators when calls were automated?

When US cities switched to dial phones between the 1920s and 1940, operator jobs for young women fell sharply. Operators over 25 were about 7 percentage points less likely to be working at all ten years later, and those still working were more likely to be in lower-paying jobs.7

Younger women overall found other work, in typing and secretarial jobs and in lower-wage service work. The cost landed hardest on the people already in the job.

What does automation do to wages?

Automation explains 50% to 70% of the change in the US wage structure between 1980 and 2016, mostly through falling relative pay for workers whose routine tasks were automated.8

For AI chatbots so far, a large Danish study found no measurable effect on average earnings or hours, up or down.10 The gains aren't reaching paycheques on their own. That's the case for rule 8.

Which jobs will AI replace by 2030?

Nobody can name them with confidence, and be wary of anyone who does. Research measures which tasks AI could do, not which jobs employers will cut. Jobs made mostly of tasks AI can do on its own, with little need for human judgment, are the most exposed.

What history does show: the people most at risk are those already in a job when it changes, especially later in their careers. That's who rule 9 is for.

Does AI help the people who use it?

Sometimes a lot, sometimes not at all, and sometimes it makes things worse. It depends on the task.

  • Support desk: 5,172 agents at one Fortune 500 software company resolved 15% more issues per hour with an AI assistant. The lowest-skilled fifth gained about 36%. The most experienced barely sped up and their quality dipped slightly.1
  • Breast screening, Sweden: AI sorted mammograms and flagged areas, and radiologists made every call. An early analysis of 80,033 women found similar cancer detection with 44% less screen reading. With 105,934 women, the AI-supported group detected 29% more cancers. In 2026, cancers missed between screenings were no more common with AI (1.55 vs 1.76 per 1,000), which the authors class as no worse, not proven better.2
  • Consultants: on tasks AI was good at, consultants finished more work faster with higher quality. On a task it was weak at, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to get it right.4
  • Experienced programmers: took 19% longer on real tasks when allowed AI tools, while believing they'd been about 20% faster.3
