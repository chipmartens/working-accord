Machines take the repetition. People keep the judgment.

Ten rules for sharing work with AI. A public commitment any employer can sign, and any worker can hold them to.

Lewis Hine, Power house mechanic working on steam pump, 1920

Every machine that took a job came with the same promise: the work comes back. It did. Not for the weaver. Not for the switchboard operator. Not for the bookkeeping clerk. Some jobs will go to AI too. This time, we write down what those people are owed before it happens.

Cutting jobs is not a reason to use AI.

If the business case is a smaller payroll, it isn't a plan. Decide what the work should look like first. Then decide who does it, and what the people it changes are owed: notice, retraining, and a share of what the software saves.

The ten rules

Written for the person on the line and the person signing the purchase order. Employers sign all ten, or none.

  1. 01

    Machines take the repetition. People keep the judgment.

    Repetitive work is real work, and for some people it is the whole job. When a machine takes it on, the person doing it is owed rules 8 and 9.

  2. 02

    A person signs off on anything that matters.

    Safety, money, health and people's jobs. The person signing can overrule the machine, gets time to check its work, and is never penalized for saying no.

  3. 03

    Questioning the tool is part of the job.

    A tool can be wrong, unsafe, a privacy risk, or a bad fit for the work. Saying so, with reasons, is doing the job well.

  4. 04

    Show the work, the machine's and yours.

    Say what the software did and what you did. Nobody passes off a machine's draft as their own thinking, starting with the people in charge.

  5. 05

    Keep the skill to check it.

    AI is strong at some tasks and confidently wrong at others. New hires learn the job itself, not just how to prompt for it.

  6. 06

    No secret scorekeeping.

    No one is disciplined or laid off because software ranked them. Where it watches or ranks people at all, they're told first and shown what it said.

  7. 07

    Your know-how is not free training data.

    When an experienced worker's knowledge trains the system, they're told, credited and paid for it.

  8. 08

    Time saved gets shared, in writing.

    At least half of freed hours go back to the people doing the work: higher pay, paid training, or better work. Reported every quarter.

  9. 09

    If a job is going to change or go, the person hears first.

    At least 90 days' notice, or the legal minimum if longer. First claim on any new role. Paid retraining. Severance on top, not instead.

  10. 10

    A headcount number is not a plan.

    A layoff lands on people and families, not on a spreadsheet. Decide what the work should look like before deciding who does it.

What the rules look like on the job

Line inspector, food plant

Used to pull defects off the line by eye for a whole shift. Now a camera flags them, and the inspector decides what actually gets pulled. When the camera misses something, she teaches it, and that teaching is part of her paid job.

Maintenance tech

Used to find out a bearing failed when the line stopped. Now gets a flag that vibration is trending up, and decides whether to pull the line at lunch or wait for Friday. The tech can ignore the flag if it's wrong.

Rules 2 and 3

Illustration. A job that goes away.

Order-entry clerk

Keys about 300 orders a day from emails and PDFs. The system starts reading the orders, and the data-entry role goes away.

Under the accord, the clerk is told 90 days before rollout, gets paid retraining on the orders the system gets wrong with first claim on that role, and severance either way.

Rule 9

Study

Support desk, one software company

5,172 agents got an AI assistant and resolved 15% more issues per hour on average. The lowest-skilled fifth gained the most, about 36%. The most experienced barely sped up, and their quality dipped slightly.

The assistant learned from the best agents' own conversations. Nothing in the study says they were paid more for it.

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2025. One Fortune 500 firm.1

Rule 7

Study

Breast screening in Sweden

AI sorted mammograms and flagged areas, and radiologists made every call. An early analysis found similar detection with 44% less screen reading. With about 106,000 women, the AI-supported group found 29% more cancers, and a 2026 follow-up found missed cancers no more common.

Less reading means fewer radiologist hours per screen. A health system can screen more women, or employ fewer radiologists.

Lång 2023, Hernström 2025, Gommers 2026.2

Rule 8

Study. Where it didn't help.

Experienced programmers

16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer on real tasks when allowed AI tools, while believing they'd been about 20% faster.

METR, 2025, which now calls the result dated for newer tools.3

Rule 5

The work comes back. Not always for the same people.

New tools create more work than they destroy. History is clear on that. It's just as clear that the new work rarely goes to the people who lost the old work.

  • 20s to 6s

    Handloom weavers, 1806 to 1830

    Weekly pay fell from about 20 shillings to about 6, and real wages to about a quarter of the peak.5 Wages across Britain took about two generations to catch up with output.6

  • 7 pts

    Telephone operators, 1920s to 1940

    Operators over 25 were about 7 points less likely to be working ten years after dial phones arrived, and those working were more likely in lower-paying jobs.7

  • 50 to 70%

    Routine-task workers, 1980 to 2016

    Automation explains 50% to 70% of the change in the US wage structure, mostly through falling relative pay for people whose tasks were automated.8

  • 19%

    Young workers, now

    In the most AI-exposed US jobs, employment of 22 to 25 year olds has fallen 19% behind less-exposed jobs in the latest data, up from 13% in the first release, mostly through less hiring.9

  • 0

    Pay, so far

    A large Danish study found AI chatbots had no measurable effect yet on average earnings or hours.10 The gains aren't reaching paycheques on their own.

Who should be worried

Not the people doing the work. These people.

  • Anyone whose AI plan is a headcount number.

    Cut first and you find out later what the people you let go actually knew: which supplier fudges specs, which machine runs hot in July, which customer is about to leave.

  • Managers who let software do the ranking.

    A score you can't explain is not a reason to discipline anyone. Rule 6 ends that.

  • Employers who won't pay for training.

    In a study of 5,172 support agents, the least-skilled gained the most.1 Those are the people a training budget is for. Cut it, and the people the tools could help most are left to figure it out alone.

  • Leaders who stopped learning.

    When work changes, the people making the decisions have to learn fastest. A boss who can't say what the software does can't tell when it's wrong, or explain a decision to the person it lands on.

  • Anyone hiding what the software does.

    If you can't tell people what it's for, you already know what they'd say. Rules 4 and 6.

People have worried about this before, often with good reason

Several of these worries came true for the people who had them, even when things worked out for everyone else. Read the full history.

  1. 370 BC

    Writing will weaken memory.

    We kept writing. He had a point: most of us no longer memorize long texts.

    Plato11

  2. 1492

    Printing will end copying by hand.

    Hand-copying did fade. Books reached millions who had never owned one.

    Trithemius12

  3. 1811

    Machines will wreck weavers' pay.

    It came true for them. The Luddites fought over wages and who got the gains, and were put down by force.

    Hobsbawm13

  4. 1920

    Dial phones will end operators' jobs.

    They mostly did. Older operators were hit hardest. Younger women moved into other work.

    Feigenbaum and Gross7

  5. 1930

    "Technological unemployment."

    Keynes called it "a temporary phase of maladjustment." Temporary can still mean years for a family.

    Keynes14

  6. 1966

    Automation will cause mass unemployment.

    A US commission found "technology eliminates jobs, not work," and recommended public jobs, a guaranteed income and education for displaced workers.

    Bowen commission15

  7. 1988

    ATMs will end bank tellers.

    Not at first: fewer tellers per branch, but many more branches. Now the US projects about 13% fewer tellers over the next decade.

    Bessen; BLS16

  8. 1980s

    Spreadsheets will end accounting work.

    400,000 clerk jobs went; 600,000 accounting jobs arrived. The clerks were mostly women. The source doesn't say where they went.

    NPR; Women's Bureau17

  9. 2018

    All of the above.

    About 60% of US jobs had titles that didn't exist in 1940. The middle has thinned.

    Autor et al.18

If your workplace is changing, ask these

Ask for the answers in writing. If you have a union, bring these to your rep. The full worker guide.

  1. Which tasks will the software take on, and whose jobs are they?
  2. Who signs off on what it does, and can they overrule it?
  3. Will it monitor, rate or rank me? Will I see what it says?
  4. If it saves hours, where do they go, and who decides?
  5. If my role changes or goes: how much notice, what training, what severance?
