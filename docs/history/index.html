People have worried about this before, often with good reason

Every major work technology, from the power loom to the ATM, came with fears that it would destroy jobs. Over decades, new work always appeared. But several of those fears came true for the people who had them: the Luddite weavers, the telephone operators, and the bookkeeping clerks all paid real costs while others gained.

Who were the Luddites, and what did they actually want?

The Luddites were English textile workers who broke machines between 1811 and 1816. They weren't against technology as such. The historian Eric Hobsbawm called machine-breaking "collective bargaining by riot": a way to pressure employers over wages and working conditions at a time when unions were illegal.13

Today "Luddite" is used to mean someone who fears new technology. The original Luddites were fighting over who got the gains from it. They were put down by force, including executions.

Were the Luddites right?

About their own lives, largely yes. Handloom weavers' weekly pay fell from about 20 shillings in 1806 to about 6 by 1830.5 Across Britain, wages lagged far behind output for roughly two generations before catching up in the second half of the 1800s.6 Their grandchildren were better off. They weren't.

What happened to telephone operators?

Between the 1920s and 1940, AT&T automated more than half of the US telephone network with dial switching. Most young women's operator jobs disappeared. Operators over 25 were about 7 percentage points less likely to be working at all ten years later, and those still working were more likely to be in lower-paying jobs. Later generations of young women found other work, much of it lower-wage.7

Did ATMs reduce the number of bank tellers?

Not at first. Between 1988 and 2004, tellers per urban bank branch fell from 20 to 13, but banks opened 43% more urban branches, so teller jobs held.16 Then the decline came. US teller jobs fell from about 600,500 in 2008 to 339,200 in 2025, and the government projects about 13% fewer over the next decade.

The ATM story is often told as proof that automation never costs jobs. It's better read as proof that the effect can arrive late.

Did spreadsheets end accounting jobs?

Bookkeeping jobs, partly. Since 1980, about 400,000 US bookkeeping and accounting clerk jobs went away, and about 600,000 accounting jobs were added.17 The clerks were mostly women: in 1980 it was one of the ten most common jobs for American women, and it is still about 86% women today. The show didn't say where they went, and we couldn't find anyone who tracked it. The new accounting jobs usually required a degree.

What is technological unemployment?

Technological unemployment is job loss caused by labour-saving technology arriving faster than new uses for labour. John Maynard Keynes described it in 1930 as "a temporary phase of maladjustment."14 He was right about the direction. But temporary can still mean years, or a career, for the people living through it.

Did people fear writing and printing too?

Yes. In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates warns that writing will "produce forgetfulness" in those who learn it.11 In 1492 the abbot Johannes Trithemius argued hand-copying was better than printing, though he didn't oppose printing itself.12 Both had a point: we no longer memorize long texts, and hand-copying did fade. Both also missed what the new tool made possible.

The pattern

Three things repeat across 200 years:

  1. The work comes back. New kinds of jobs have always appeared, often ones nobody predicted.
  2. It doesn't come back for the same people. The cost falls on whoever is in the job when it changes, especially later in their careers.
  3. Who gets the gains is a choice. It was decided by laws, unions and employers, not by the machines.
