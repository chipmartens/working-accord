Bringing in AI without losing your people

Tell people early, decide who signs off, share what the software saves, and give anyone whose job changes notice, retraining and severance. That's most of the accord. The rest of this page turns the ten rules into a checklist you can use before any AI project is approved.

The difference between a change that lifts people and one that frightens them is rarely the software. It comes down to whether people are told early, trained properly, and given a share of what the change saves.

Before you buy

  • Name the tasks. List which tasks the software will do and which decisions stay with people. Note whose jobs those tasks belong to. (Rule 1)
  • Don't approve on a headcount number. The plan names the work, the people affected, and how you'll meet rules 8 and 9. (Rule 10)
  • Tell people now. Before the decision is final, in writing, with a real way to raise concerns. (Rule 3)
  • Check monitoring. If the tool can watch, rate or rank people, decide now whether you'll use that, and plan to tell them. (Rule 6)

During rollout

  • Name the person who signs off on anything touching safety, money, health or jobs, and give them the time and authority to say no. (Rule 2)
  • Agree the time split in writing. At least half of saved hours go to higher pay, paid training or better work. (Rule 8)
  • Pay for know-how. If experienced staff's work trains the system, tell them, credit them and pay them. (Rule 7)
  • Give notice early. At least 90 days, or the legal minimum if longer, before any role changes or ends, with retraining and first claim on new roles. (Rule 9)

After it's running

  • Report the time split to the team every quarter.
  • Keep the skill. Train new hires on the job itself, and practise without the tool often enough to catch its mistakes. (Rule 5)
  • Show the work. Make it normal to say what the software produced and what people added. (Rule 4)

What does the law already require?

Employment standards set minimum notice or pay for terminations, with longer group notice when 50 or more people are let go within four weeks: 10 to 18 weeks in Manitoba, 8 to 16 weeks in Ontario, and at least 16 weeks for federally regulated employers. Since January 1, 2026, Ontario employers with 25 or more employees must say in public job postings if AI is used to screen, assess or select applicants (Osler summary). The Privacy Commissioner says monitoring should be "specific, targeted and appropriate" (guidance). Unionized workplaces may have technological change clauses. The accord sets a floor above all of these; get legal advice for your situation.

Signing the accord

Employers sign all ten rules, or don't sign. No picking the easy ones. Signers are listed with the date. If a worker reports a breach, we ask you to answer, and we note on the list that a question was raised and whether you answered.
